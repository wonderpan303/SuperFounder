1

Your first one will be your worst one.

So let's get it out of the way. Today.

Instead of thinking

"I'll post when I feel ready."

Think this

"Ready comes from doing it. My first one is supposed to be bad."

Why it helps: Nobody feels ready before their first video. Confidence shows up after you've done something a few times, not before. Nervous and capable can live in the same body.

Do this

Step 1 30 seconds

Shake it loose

Stand up. Shake your hands, then your arms, then your legs for about 20 seconds. Then stand still and feel both feet on the floor for 10.

You're not trying to get calm. You're burning off the extra nervous energy.

0:30
Step 2 1 minute

Turn the camera on before you're ready

Play your favorite song. Turn your camera on, hit record, and let it roll for a few seconds while you look at yourself and enjoy the song.

Then turn the music off, and start talking.

Look into the lens (the little circle, not your face on the screen) and say "Hello, this is practice" three times. Slower each time.

Why I start with music, then turn it off

The music is just the warm-up, not the background. Your favorite song tells your body this is a party, not a threat. By the time you turn it off to speak, your body has already decided the camera is safe.

Step 3 1 minute

Record your worst one, on purpose

Hit record and finish this sentence out loud:

"The thing most people get wrong about [your field] is…"

  1. One take. Don't restart.
  2. Save it. Don't delete it for at least 30 minutes.
  3. Don't rewatch it yet. Today is about finishing, not judging.
That's rep one. It only goes up from here.

Step 1 1 minute

Hand on heart. Sigh it out.

Put your hand on your heart. Breathe in through your nose for 4. Hold for a moment. Then let out one big sigh. Follow the circle for 6 breaths.

Nervous means bring your energy down, not up. When you're calm, people see someone with nothing to prove and nothing to hide. If you feel light-headed, go back to normal breathing.

    3

    It was never about you.

    It's about the people you're here to help. Make it about them, and the nerves have nowhere to live.

    The real problem

    When you're on camera, all your attention is on you. How do I look? How do I sound? Am I good enough? That's exactly where nerves grow.

    So flip the focus onto the people you're here to help. The ones who need what you know, and whose day, business or life could change because you shared it.

    Instead of thinking

    "I need to sound smart."

    Think this

    "I need the person I'm helping to feel seen and understood."

    Why it helps: Your nerves shrink when your attention moves off yourself and onto one person you're helping. And people keep watching when they feel seen and understood, not when you sound impressive.

    What yapping actually is

    Yapping is simply using what you know to change someone else's life. One shift, for one person, in that moment.

    How to make people want to watch

    1

    Pick one person

    A real person, by name, who needs what you know. Talk only to that one person.

    e.g. Maya, who's about to launch her first offer
    2

    Name what they feel, first

    Your first sentence says what they're feeling right now. That sentence is your hook. When people feel seen, they stay.

    e.g. "If you've rewritten the same post five times and still haven't posted it, this is for you."
    3

    Say one idea in one sentence

    Before you record, say your main point in one sentence. If you can't, it's not ready to film yet.

    e.g. "Launch to 10 people before you launch to everyone."

    The whole routine at a glance

      If you freeze in the middle

      Went blank? Breathe out once. Look at your first point. Say "The main point is…" and keep going.

      Rambling? Say "The short version is…" then say one sentence and stop.

      Talking too fast? Slow down. Give the important words a punch and pause.

      Made a mistake? Say "Let me say that more clearly." No apology needed.

      That's a routine, not a mood. Run it again tomorrow.

      The last stop decides whether you're still doing this three weeks from now.

      None of this holds without your why.

      The moment it gets hard, and it will, the reframes alone won't keep you in the room.

      Instead of thinking

      "I'll stay motivated."

      "Motivation fades. My why doesn't."

      Why it helps: Most people don't quit because they're bad on camera. They quit in week three because they forgot why they started. Your why is the real reason you're doing this. The one that keeps you going when it gets hard.

      Where this comes from

      This is a short version of the "7 Levels Deep" exercise, made famous by entrepreneur Dean Graziosi. He learned it from coach Joe Stumpf, who used it with his high-level clients. It's the same keep-asking-why idea behind Toyota's famous "5 Whys" for finding the root of a problem. Here, the problem is quitting, and the root is your why.
